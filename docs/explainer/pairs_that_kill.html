Pairs that kill: choosing which human genes to test first

Some genes are harmless to lose one at a time but deadly to lose together. Finding those pairs could point to new cancer treatments, but there are far too many pairs to test them all. We used a complete map from yeast to work out how human experiments should choose where to look.

Two knockouts, one dead cell

A cell can usually survive losing a single gene, because another gene covers for it. Synthetic lethality is when two genes back each other up: knock out either one and the cell lives, knock out both and it dies.

This matters for medicine. Many tumours have already lost one gene through mutation. A drug that blocks its partner would kill the tumour cells and largely spare healthy cells, which still have both genes. PARP inhibitors for BRCA-mutant breast and ovarian cancers work this way.

The problem is scale

~18,000human protein-coding genes in a standard genome-wide knockout library
~160 millionpossible pairs of those genes (18,000 × 18,000 ÷ 2)
~1 in 100tested pairs that show a strong harmful interaction, in both yeast and human cells

Testing pairs one at a time is impossible at this scale. Instead, labs use a screen. They build one cell line that lacks a chosen "query" gene, then use CRISPR to knock out every other gene in it, all at once in a pool. One screen tests one query gene against all ~18,000 others. Each query line has to be engineered and grown in large numbers, so a lab can afford hundreds of screens, not thousands.

The biggest human map to date (Billmann, Costanzo and colleagues, Cell 2026) screened 222 query genes in a human cell line called HAP1. It tested about 3.9 million pairs and found about 47,000 harmful interactions. The authors estimate this covers under 5% of all possible pairs. So which query genes should be screened next?

Learning from yeast, where the whole map exists

We can't test strategies in human cells without the answers. Baker's yeast is different. Its map is essentially complete for genes that are not essential: 3,283 query genes were each screened against 3,827 others, about 12.6 million measured combinations, of which about 129,000 are strongly harmful (Costanzo and colleagues, Science 2016).

That lets us rehearse the human problem with the answer key in hand:

  • We locked away 656 yeast genes (20%) as an exam that no method was allowed to see or screen.
  • We then "screened" other genes, in a simulation, either chosen at random or chosen smartly in rounds of 10.
  • After each round, a computer model used the screened results, plus gene information that also exists for humans (activity levels, protein partners, evolutionary conservation), to predict the harmful partners of the locked-away genes.
  • We scored how well those predictions ranked the true partners.

The locked-away genes were scored exactly once, at the very end, after every choice had been frozen. An independent re-check of all the numbers found no discrepancies.

1. More screens keep helping, with no plateau

With randomly chosen query genes, predictions get steadily better as more genes are screened. They never level off. Screening 20% of genes at random gives predictions about 6.7× better than guessing. Screening everything we could gives about 9.8×.

2. Choosing smartly gets you more from the same budget

With 160 screens (5% of genes), the smart strategies made predictions about 26–31% better than random screening. This was the main pre-registered test, and it passed for all three smart strategies. To match them, random screening needed about 430 genes, roughly 2.5 times as many.

To be clear about the limits: 160 smart screens did not match 660 random screens (20%). They closed about 55–65% of that gap. They also did not clearly beat 490 random screens.

The smart strategies work by spending early screens on genes that turn out to have many partners. Their results reveal a lot about the rest of the map. The gain is largest for genes with many harmful partners. For genes with few, it is small.

3. What makes a good gene to screen

The genes the smart strategies picked had a clear profile. Each feature can be measured for human genes too:

  • Losing the gene alone already makes the cell grow poorly. This was the strongest signal.
  • It is conserved across species.
  • It is steadily and highly active, with many protein partners.

A simple fixed rule using just three features (poor growth when the gene is lost, conservation, and steady activity) recovered most of the smart strategies' advantage in a follow-up check. That check is promising but small (3 runs), so it needs confirming. By contrast, a panel chosen for diversity, spread across growth rates and activity levels in the way the human study chose its genes, did no better than random.

What computer predictions can and cannot do

Predictions do not replace experiments, but they narrow the search. After screening about 20% of genes smartly, each unscreened gene's top 10 predicted partners contain about one true harmful partner. That is 10–14 times better than picking 10 at random. At that point the predictions are about as reliable as re-measuring the same pair in reverse. They remain far less reliable than an actual screen.

One warning for labs. When genes are chosen smartly, the usual way to check a model, by testing it on the genes already screened, badly overstates how good it is (by up to 2.4 times). That makes it unreliable for deciding when to stop. It is better to fix the number of rounds in advance.

Our recommendation for human screens

  1. Start with the right kind of gene. For the first round, pick query genes whose loss alone slows growth, that are conserved, and that are steadily active. Not a deliberately diverse panel.
  2. Screen in a few large rounds and re-choose between them. After each round of about 300 lines, use the results to rank the next candidates.
  3. Plan for about 1,000–1,500 query lines to reach 80–90% of whole-genome prediction quality. Random choice would need about 3,300–4,900. Every extra 100 lines keeps adding value.
  4. If a diverse panel already exists, switch strategy now. In our tests, switching after an existing 220-gene panel gave 27–40% better predictions than continuing it.
  5. Fix the number of rounds in advance. Don't stop based on how well the model predicts the genes already screened.

One practical caution: the best candidates are often the hardest cell lines to make, because losing the gene already makes cells sick. Leaving out the sickest 10% cost a little (6–9% of the benefit) in our tests.

Caveats

  • This is a rehearsal in yeast. Individual harmful pairs rarely carry over from yeast to human. Network-level patterns do carry over better, such as some genes having many partners, so the advice is about how many genes to screen and what kind, not which specific genes.
  • The human numbers rest on a scaling estimate and on an assumed number of human query genes. Different reasonable assumptions shift them by roughly ±30%, and the most optimistic one would cut the random-choice figure to about 1,200 lines.
  • The yeast map excludes essential genes, which human screens include.
  • Measurements are noisy. Even the yeast map agrees with itself only partly when a pair is measured in both directions, which caps how good any prediction can look.

How this was done. All choices were fixed in advance using development data only. The 656 locked-away yeast genes were evaluated in a single, one-time run, and the results were recomputed independently with separate code.
