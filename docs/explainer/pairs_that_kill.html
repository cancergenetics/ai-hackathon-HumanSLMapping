Which queries to screen next? Designing human synthetic-lethal maps from the yeast network

Query-by-genome CRISPR screens in HAP1 cells now map genetic interactions at scale, but each query line is expensive and the pair space is enormous. We used the essentially complete yeast SGA network as a testbed to ask how many queries are needed, which ones to pick, and how much computational prediction can fill in for unscreened genes.

The target: strong negative interactions

We focus on synthetic lethal / strong negative genetic interactions. These are pairs where the double mutant is much sicker than the single mutants predict: ε < −0.12 at P < 0.05 in the yeast SGA scoring. They are the interactions most directly useful for target discovery, where a tumour's loss-of-function lesion creates a dependency on its buffering partner. PARP inhibition in BRCA1/2-deficient tumours is the textbook case. They are also sparse: about 1.1% of tested pairs in yeast. HAP1 has a comparable negative-interaction density of about 1.2%.

The design problem is the pair space

~18,000genes in a genome-wide library (TKOv3), ~11,000 of them expressed in HAP1
~160 Mpossible gene pairs in the library (~60 M among expressed genes)
<5%of possible HAP1 pairs assayed so far (Billmann et al., 2026)

A query screen measures one query knockout against the whole library, so it reveals one row of the interaction matrix. The cost unit is the query line: each must be engineered, validated and screened in replicate. The largest human map to date (Billmann, Costanzo et al., Cell 2026) screened 222 HAP1 query lines (298 screens). It tested about 3.9 M pairs and called 47,052 negative interactions. Its queries were chosen for functional diversity across expression and fitness, not for network coverage.

The open questions are therefore experimental-design questions:

  • How does predictive coverage of unscreened genes scale with the number of queries?
  • Does choosing queries adaptively, in batches, beat random or diversity-driven panels at the same budget?
  • What does that imply for the number and identity of the next HAP1 query lines?

To our knowledge, iterative query selection has not been evaluated for genetic interaction maps. Related active-learning work exists for Perturb-seq and for drug–target matrices.

Testbed: the yeast NxN network with an answer key

The Costanzo et al. (2016) nonessential × nonessential map gives a near-complete matrix: 3,283 queries × 3,827 arrays, about 12.6 M scored pairs and 128,955 strong negatives. We used it to simulate HAP1-style campaigns.

  • Held-out test queries. Before any development, 656 query genes (20%) were held out, stratified by interaction degree. They were never screened, selected or used for tuning.
  • Observation model. Screening a gene reveals its full query row. Any pair involving a screened gene counts as observed, on whichever side. AB/BA measurements are kept as separate directed cells, because their concordance is low (κ ≈ 0.14).
  • Predictor. A kernel-ridge fold-in of the screened interaction profiles, stacked with logistic regression. Gene features are limited to sources with a direct human analogue: coexpression, AP-MS interactome, ESM-2 protein embeddings, paralogy and conservation, abundance, and single-mutant fitness. There are no external genetic interactions, no text-mining or STRING, and no GO, to avoid circularity.
  • Selection strategies, run in batches of 10:
    • random;
    • a Billmann-like diversity panel stratified by fitness and expression;
    • adaptive hub-first (H), a degree model refit on screened rows;
    • coverage (C), facility location in the kernel space;
    • Fisher D-optimal design over low-rank interaction factors (F);
    • static, one-shot versions of these.
  • Evaluation. AUPRC for strong negatives on held-out query rows × never-screenable arrays. This set is identical for every strategy, so all comparisons are paired.
  • Pre-registration. The analysis plan, finalist strategies and statistical tests were frozen before the test set was touched. It was run once and recomputed by independent code.

1. Random query sampling: steady gains, no saturation

With randomly sampled queries, AUPRC on held-out queries rises from 0.031 at 30 queries to 0.042 at 160 (5%), 0.063 at 660 (20%) and 0.092 with all 2,627 candidate queries screened. That is a lift over the 0.94% base rate of 3.2×, 4.5×, 6.7× and 9.8×. The curve has not saturated. Reaching 80% / 90% of the full-pool AUPRC takes about 1,140 / 1,760 queries, i.e. 35% / 54% of all 3,283 query genes.

The model matters as much as the data. Simple degree baselines plateau by about 120 queries, while the full predictor keeps improving. Its advantage over a simpler stacked model grows from +14% at 160 queries to +36% at 660.

2. Adaptive selection beats random at matched budget

At 160 queries (5%), all three pre-specified adaptive strategies outperformed random sampling (one-sided tests, Holm-corrected, p ≤ 0.0014):

  • hub-first: +31% AUPRC;
  • coverage: +31%;
  • Fisher design: +26%.

Random sampling needs about 380–430 queries to reach the same AUPRC, a 2.4–2.7× budget equivalence. At 330–490 queries this rises to 3–4×.

Limits: 160 adaptive queries did not match 660 random queries (the "5% vs 20%" question). They closed 55–65% of that gap, and they did not significantly beat 490 random queries. The gain is concentrated in pooled AUPRC on high-degree targets: per-query (macro) AUPRC improves only 5–10%, and AUROC is unchanged. The Billmann-like diversity panel was indistinguishable from random at every budget.

Mechanism. Adaptive strategies preferentially screen moderately high-degree genes (about 1.5–1.6× the degree of random picks), not the extreme hubs. Their rows reveal 1.4–1.6× more strong negatives, including interactions in the array-side columns of unscreened genes, and the fold-in predictor exploits these. A true-degree "oracle" that screens the biggest hubs first does worse, because it overshoots. Selection is leak-free: selections were bit-identical when all held-out data were scrambled.

3. Which genes make informative queries

The early adaptive picks share a profile, and every feature in it has a human counterpart (HAP1/DepMap single-KO fitness, expression atlases, orthology, BioPlex/OpenCell):

  • Low single-mutant fitness: about −1.7 SD vs the candidate pool. This was the dominant signal.
  • High conservation and paralog identity.
  • High, stable expression and higher AP-MS degree.

These are the same correlates of interaction degree reported for HAP1 hubs. In a post-hoc check, a static three-feature ranking (low fitness, high conservation, low expression variability) recovered 91–108% of the adaptive gain on held-out genes. The ranking was fixed on development data before testing, but the check used only 3 runs with no CI, so it needs confirmation. The pre-specified static variants recovered much less, and diversity sampling recovered nothing.

4. Translating to HAP1 query numbers

Subsampling the yeast gene universe gave a scaling exponent of b ≈ 1.06 [0.85, 1.30] for queries needed versus universe size. In other words, the queries required scale with the fraction of genes, not their absolute count. What prediction adds, and a caution on model validation

After about 20% of queries chosen adaptively, each unscreened gene's top-10 predicted partners contain about one true strong negative on average, 10–14× above chance. On on-array targets, precision at matched recall (0.22) roughly equals the agreement between reciprocal AB/BA measurements of the same pair (0.18). It remains far below a HAP1 screen's own precision of 0.61 at recall 0.37. Prediction prioritises; it does not replace screening.

Leave-batch-out cross-validation on screened queries is a poor progress monitor under adaptive selection. The screened rows are enriched for high-degree genes, so CV AUPRC overestimated held-out performance by up to 2.4×. A plateau-based stopping rule, frozen in advance, did no better than a fixed budget. Fix the number of rounds up front, or reserve a small random validation set of queries.

Recommendations for the next HAP1 campaign

  1. Seed the first round with informative queries. Rank candidates by low single-KO fitness, high conservation and stable expression, not by functional diversity.
  2. Re-rank between rounds. After each round of ~300 lines, refit a degree model on the screened profiles (hub-first) and choose the next batch.
  3. Budget about 1,000–1,500 query lines for 80–90% of full-pool predictive performance. Random sampling would need about 3,300–4,900.
  4. Build on existing panels. Switching to adaptive selection after a diversity panel still yields most of the benefit.
  5. Set the stopping point in advance, or validate on randomly chosen held-out queries, not on CV of the adaptive set.

Practical caveat: the most informative queries are often the sickest lines to derive. Excluding the lowest-fitness decile reduced the benefit by 6–9% in development runs.

Limitations

  • Yeast → human transfer. Pair-level conservation of interactions between yeast and HAP1 is low. Network-level properties transfer better: degree correlates and hub features. The guidance concerns how many queries and what kind, not specific pairs.
  • The HAP1 numbers are projections. They depend on the scaling exponent from random sub-universes (an approximation), on the HAP1 query universe size (7,500–9,500), and on the denominator. The b = 0 bound is the optimistic floor.
  • Essential genes are absent from the yeast NxN matrix but present in HAP1 libraries.
  • Labels are noisy. Single-direction calls agree poorly with their reciprocal measurement, which caps attainable AUPRC.
  • Hub-first, coverage and Fisher design were not ranked against each other, and their order differs between budgets.
Methods in brief.
  • Development used three internal splits of the non-test genes. The predictor, finalist strategies and analysis plan were frozen before the one-shot test evaluation (4,019 evaluation units).
  • Statistics: paired Welch contrasts on a shared stratified query bootstrap, with Holm correction over the confirmatory family. Points were recomputed by an independent rescorer with 0 mismatches.
